# **Abstract**

In Lebanon, finding a trustworthy electrician, plumber, or appliance technician still depends almost entirely on personal referral. Customers cannot compare providers systematically or verify a track record; tradespeople, in turn, remain confined to a narrow referral network and accumulate no evidence of good work. This project proposes Fixify, a mobile application that turns this fragmented, word-of-mouth market into a searchable and reputation-backed one. Customers will describe a problem in a chat-style intake and attach photographs, receive an AI-generated diagnosis that automatically recommends matching providers, inspect profiles and ratings, book one of the appointment slots the AI generates from the provider’s calendar, add that appointment to their own device calendar, and track the job through a defined lifecycle. Providers will manage the trades they offer, indicate whether they are currently accepting work, and respond to requests from a single dashboard, where each job arrives as an issue card carrying the customer’s description, photographs, diagnosis, and an editable list of the parts it needs. Communication between the two parties happens by telephone rather than in-app messaging. The system will be built with React Native, TypeScript, and MobX on the client, an Express.js REST API with token-based authentication on the server, and MySQL for data storage, following an iterative methodology across the semester. The expected outcome is a tested, demonstrable mobile system whose central contribution is a reputation model in which every review is structurally tied to a completed job.

## **Purpose and Objectives**

## The purpose of this project is to design and implement a mobile application, Fixify, that connects customers with local service providers through AI-assisted diagnosis and provider matching, category-based search, a structured service request lifecycle, and a review system. The specific objectives are to:

- **Design and implement a secure authentication system** (registration, login, password hashing with bcrypt, JWT-based session handling, and password reset) shared across customer and provider roles.

- **Develop a provider search and filtering system** that lets customers find providers by service category and service area, with results ordered by rating, callout price, or earliest available appointment.

- **Implement an AI-assisted diagnosis and matching process** in which a customer describes the problem in a chat-style input and may attach photographs; the conversation is stored in the database as history, the text and images are analysed in a single structured call that returns a diagnosis (likely fault, category, service type, estimated duration, and a short explanation) together with a suggested parts list, the customer may amend the diagnosis, and matching providers are then recommended automatically from it.

- **Build an availability and appointment-scheduling model** in which providers publish their weekly working hours, available appointment slots are generated by AI from that calendar and the diagnosed job duration rather than listed manually by the provider, every generated slot is validated by the server against existing bookings, and a booked appointment can be added to the customer’s own device calendar with one button.

- **Implement a per-job parts and materials list** in which the AI suggests the items a provider is likely to need, the provider reviews and edits that list on the job’s issue card, and the confirmed items are stored in a dedicated table linked to the job.

- **Keep direct communication off-platform**: the application provides no in-app messaging between customer and provider; instead, a call button opens the device’s phone dialer with the provider’s number.

- **Implement a quoting mechanism** allowing a provider to propose a price for a job and a customer to accept or decline it, including revision of that price when additional work is discovered during a visit.

- **Implement a structured service-request lifecycle** (Pending → Scheduled → In Progress → Completed, with Rejected and Cancelled terminal states) in which a request owns one or more visits, supporting rescheduling, follow-up visits, and multi-day work, with all transitions validated on the server.

- **Design a normalised relational database** in MySQL that enforces the core domain rules one review per completed job, no double-booked appointment slot, referential integrity between users, providers, intake conversations, requests, visits and job items through primary keys, foreign keys, unique constraints, check constraints, and a trigger, rather than through application code alone.

- **Implement an append-only event log** that records every significant action against a job, producing a chronological progress timeline for both parties and supplying the in-app notification feed.

- **Implement a review and rating system** that lets customers rate completed jobs, with each request reviewable at most once.

- **Build dedicated, role-specific mobile interfaces** for customers (describe the issue, review the diagnosis, choose a recommended provider, book a slot, add it to the device calendar, call the provider, track, review) and for providers (profile, trades and rates, working hours, issue cards with editable parts lists, quoting, visit management, ratings).

- **Deliver a working, testable mobile MVP** by the end of the semester, validated through unit testing of the request state machine and manual testing of the end-to-end flows.

##  **Significance **This project addresses a real friction point for customers and independent technicians in the local market, where people often rely on informal channels such as neighbourhood Facebook groups, WhatsApp broadcast lists, and classified sites to find service providers. Although these tools provide reach, they lack a structured service-request process, appointment scheduling, status tracking, and ratings tied to completed jobs, making it difficult for customers to compare providers or to manage a job once it has begun.

The difficulty is not only one of discovery. Even after a customer and a tradesperson have found one another, the transaction itself is unmanaged: there is no shared record of what was agreed, what was quoted, when the visit is due, or what happened when the work turned out to be larger than expected. Fixify addresses discovery and the transaction together, on the argument that a reputation system is only credible if the platform can also observe whether the job was actually completed.

The AI-assisted intake addresses a further asymmetry. A customer who is not a tradesperson typically cannot name the fault, and often cannot say which trade it belongs to; a provider, receiving only a short and imprecise description, cannot judge whether the job is worth travelling to or what to bring. Structuring the customer’s own words and photographs into a categorised, tagged brief serves both sides of that gap, and does so at the point where the information is first captured rather than after several exchanges of messages. Because the diagnosis names the trade, it can also be used to recommend suitable providers directly, so the customer does not need to know what kind of tradesperson to look for; and because it anticipates the parts involved, the provider can arrive with what the job is likely to need.

# **Development Approach**

## **Methodology**

The system will be developed as a cross-platform native mobile application for Android and iOS, supported by a REST API and a relational database. Work will be tracked on GitHub, with each feature developed on its own branch and merged through a pull request that must pass automated linting and type-checking before review.

##  **System Architecture**

The client tier is a React Native application in which MobX observable stores hold application state and presentational components remain free of business logic. An Axios client attaches the authentication token to every request in a single place. Two device integrations stand in for features that might otherwise be built inside the application: a booked appointment is written to the customer’s own calendar through the device calendar interface (expo-calendar), and contacting a provider opens the phone dialer with the provider’s number rather than an in-app chat.

The application tier is an Express.js service written in TypeScript, layered into middleware (JWT verification, schema validation, rate limiting, security headers), route controllers, a service layer holding the business rules and the request state machine, and a data-access layer containing all SQL. Dependencies point in one direction only: the data-access layer knows nothing of business rules, and the service layer knows nothing of HTTP. The practical test applied is that any service function must be callable from a script with no HTTP server running. An AI module wraps the two calls made to the language model — the intake diagnosis, which also returns the suggested parts list, and the generation of appointment slots — and each returns structured JSON that the service layer validates before it is used or stored.

The data tier is MySQL 8 using the InnoDB engine, accessed through parameterised queries. No object-relational mapper is used. This is a deliberate choice: the schema and the constraints it carries are a substantive part of the project’s contribution, and hiding them behind a generated abstraction would obscure exactly what the work is meant to demonstrate. Uploaded photographs are held in object storage, with the database retaining only a reference.

## **Functional Model and Use Cases**

The customer describes a problem in a chat-style intake and attaches photographs, reviews the AI diagnosis, chooses from the providers recommended for it (or searches by trade), books one of the AI-generated appointment slots, adds the appointment to the device calendar, tracks, confirms, and reviews. The provider maintains a portfolio of trades and rates, publishes working hours, opens the issue card for each incoming job, edits the suggested parts list, quotes, manages visits, and marks work complete. The application deliberately contains no messaging between the two parties: when the customer needs to speak to the provider, the application places a telephone call to the provider’s number. Authentication, account management, and notifications are shared between the two roles, and a single account may act in both capacities without a role flag, since provider status is conferred by the existence of a row rather than by an attribute.

## **Database Design**

The providers table takes the user identifier as both its primary key and its foreign key to users, which makes the one-to-one relationship and means that the existence of the row is what identifies someone as a provider, so a customer may also be a provider without a role flag to keep in sync. The provider_categories table uses a composite primary key over its two foreign keys, which models the many-to-many relationship and makes duplicate rows impossible without a surrogate identifier.

The reviews table stores only a reference to the request, not to the customer or provider, since both are already recorded on the request. A unique constraint on that reference guarantees at most one review per job, and a trigger rejects any review whose request is not in the completed state. Together these make the central claim of the project that every review is tied to completed work a property of the schema rather than a convention of the interface.

The visits table separates the job from the occasions on which it is worked. A request owns zero or more visits, which is what allows rescheduling, follow-up appointments, and multi-day work to be represented without introducing additional job states. The table carries the provider identifier alongside the request identifier, which is redundant in the ordinary sense but is constrained by a composite foreign key so that the two cannot disagree. That redundancy earns its place by allowing a unique constraint on the provider and a generated active_slot column, which holds the visit’s start time while the visit is live and becomes null once it is cancelled. This makes double-booking impossible at the database level rather than dependent on a check performed in application code, where it would be subject to a race between two concurrent bookings, while still allowing a cancelled slot to be booked again. A separate proposed_by reference to users records which party proposed a new time when a visit is rescheduled.

The job_events table is append-only. Rows are never updated or deleted, and it serves three purposes at once: it renders as the customer-facing progress timeline, it is the audit record for how a job reached its current state, and it supplies the notification feed. Deriving notifications from it, rather than maintaining a parallel notifications table, avoids a class of defect in which the two representations disagree.

The intake conversation is stored in three tables. intake_sessions holds one row per conversation, owned by the customer, together with the status of the AI analysis and its raw structured response; intake_messages holds the turns of the conversation in order, each marked as written by the customer or by the AI; and intake_photos holds a reference to each image attached to a customer message, the files themselves being kept in object storage. A request references the session it came from through a unique foreign key, so a diagnosis produces at most one job, while a conversation that never becomes a request is still kept as history. The values the customer confirms — category, service type, description, and estimated duration — are copied onto the request, so the request remains readable without reaching back into the model’s output, and the ai_status and ai_analysis columns previously held on requests move to the session, as ai_status and ai_diagnosis.

The request_items table holds the parts and materials the provider needs to complete a job, and its structure is given in Table 1. Each row belongs to one request. The AI’s suggestions are inserted as rows when the request is created and marked with their source; the provider may then edit, add, or remove rows from the issue card, and edited rows are re-marked as the provider’s. The saved list is the one the system relies on, while the model’s original suggestion survives unchanged in the session’s raw response, so what was proposed and what the provider changed remain distinguishable. Quantities are constrained to be positive, and any estimated price is stored as a fixed-point decimal rather than a floating-point number.

##  **Service Request Lifecycle**

The request lifecycle is the core business process. A request enters the Pending state when the customer books a slot with one of the recommended providers; the intake conversation that precedes it is kept as history but is not part of the lifecycle. All transitions are validated on the server against the state currently persisted and the acting user’s role; the client never sets a status directly, and a client’s claim about the current state is never trusted. Only a completed request unlocks the review form, which is the mechanism implementing the verified-reputation guarantee.

Quote state is modelled separately from job state. A price moves from none to proposed, and then to accepted or declined, on its own axis, because a revised quote may be issued while the job is already in progress. Encoding quote status as a job status would create two representations of the same fact and permit them to diverge.

## **Visits, Rescheduling, and Follow-up Work**

Separating the job from its visits resolves a set of situations that a single-appointment model cannot express. If the work runs long, the provider extends the current visit. If a part is unavailable, the provider proposes a second visit and the job remains open. If either party needs to move an appointment, one proposes a new time and the other confirms it, and the change is recorded as an event rather than as a new state. A warranty callback is simply a further visit against the same job.

Work discovered during a visit is handled by the quoting mechanism rather than by a separate feature: the provider issues a revised price with a stated reason and a supporting photograph, and the customer approves or declines it in the application. The original and revised prices both appear in the job’s event history.

**4.7 AI Diagnosis and Provider Matching**

The customer begins by describing the problem in a chat-style input — for example, “water leaking under my kitchen sink” — and may attach photographs. Every turn of this conversation, with its images, is saved to the database against an intake session, so the customer can return to it later and the provider sees exactly what was described. The text and images are sent to a language model in a single structured call, which returns one object containing a likely fault with a confidence level, a service category, a service type, an estimated duration, a short plain-language explanation, an urgency assessment, descriptive tags, an indicative price range, and a suggested list of parts. The customer sees this as a compact diagnosis, for instance “Likely P-trap leak · Plumbing · Repair · ~90 min”, followed by the explanation “This usually means a loose or worn seal under the sink. A plumber can typically fix it in one visit.”

The diagnosis then drives provider matching automatically. The server selects providers who offer the diagnosed category in the customer’s area and are currently accepting work, and ranks them by rating and by the earliest available slot long enough for the estimated duration; the customer chooses from this recommended list rather than having to know which trade to search for. The matching itself is an ordinary database query: the model supplies its inputs, not its ranking, which keeps the recommendations explainable and repeatable. Category search remains available to a customer who already knows what they want or wishes to look further.

Three constraints govern this feature. First, the model proposes and the person confirms: the diagnosis is presented to the customer as an editable suggestion, and the customer’s corrections, not the model’s output, are what the request retains. Second, analysis never blocks the customer: if the model is slow or unavailable, the customer chooses a category manually and continues through ordinary search, so a failure degrades the experience without preventing use of the application. Third, the raw response is retained on the intake session as an audit record, so that what the model proposed and what the human changed both remain inspectable.

The feature is included because it performs work that would otherwise fall to a human dispatcher triaging an unstructured complaint into a trade, a job type, a list of likely parts, and a shortlist of suitable providers and not for the sake of incorporating a language model. Its output is treated throughout as a proposal to be confirmed, which is also what makes it safe to rely on.

## **4.8 Availability and Appointment Selection**

Each provider publishes a weekly working pattern, which together with the visits already booked makes up that provider’s calendar availability. Providers do not list appointment slots themselves. Instead, the slots offered to the customer are generated by AI: the model receives the provider’s free windows over a forward period and the duration estimated at diagnosis, and returns a small set of appointment slots sized to the job and spread across the coming days, rather than every fixed interval the calendar would allow. Before any slot is shown, the server checks it against the provider’s working hours and existing visits and discards any that conflict, and the unique constraint on provider and active slot in the visits table remains the final guarantee against double-booking. If the AI step fails, the server falls back to offering the free windows directly.

Once a slot is booked, the job screen shows an “Add to calendar” button. It writes the appointment — time, expected duration, address, the diagnosis title, and the provider’s name and phone number — to the customer’s own device calendar through the operating system’s calendar interface, after asking for calendar permission. The application keeps no calendar of its own for customers; if the appointment is later rescheduled, the button is offered again for the new time.

Availability is declared on one side only. A design in which both parties declare availability and the system computes an intersection was considered and rejected: it roughly doubles the implementation cost, produces a materially identical interface, and introduces a failure state in which no overlap exists and the customer cannot proceed.

## **4.9 Provider Issue Card and Parts List**

When a customer selects a provider and books a slot, the job reaches that provider as an issue card. The card shows the customer’s own description, the AI diagnosis, and the photographs attached during intake, so the provider can judge the job before accepting it without asking the customer to repeat anything. It also shows the parts and materials the AI expects the job to need — for the leak above, perhaps a replacement P-trap washer and a slip nut — as an editable list.

The provider can change quantities, remove items that do not apply, and add items the model missed. Saving writes the list to the request_items table against that job (Table 1), and the change is recorded in the job’s event history. As with the diagnosis, the suggestion is a starting point rather than a commitment: the saved list is the provider’s, and the model’s original suggestion is kept only in the audit record.

## ** Usability, Performance, and Scalability**

The application targets a broad, non-technical audience that includes tradespeople with limited experience of app interfaces, so the design favours large touch targets, a shallow navigation hierarchy, and few steps per task. The target is that a new customer can move from opening the application to submitting a request in under two minutes without assistance, measured during acceptance testing.

# **9. Anticipated Impact**

For customers, the anticipated impact is a measurable reduction in the time and uncertainty of engaging a tradesperson, replacing a serial telephone search with a diagnosis of the problem, a shortlist of matched providers, and a scheduled appointment that sits in their own calendar. For providers, it is access to demand beyond an existing referral network, together with a reputation record they own and carry forward, and this should also exert a mild disciplining effect on quality, since a public rating history makes poor work costly.

The intake mechanism carries a further benefit for providers specifically. An issue card that identifies the likely fault, shows the customer’s photographs, and carries a parts list the provider has confirmed reduces wasted journeys and allows a provider to judge a job before committing to it a cost that falls almost entirely on the tradesperson under the present arrangement.

# **10. Conclusion**

This proposal has set out the design for Fixify, a mobile marketplace connecting customers with local skilled service providers. The problem it addresses arises in a market where the great majority of employment is informal, customers cannot systematically find or evaluate tradespeople, and tradespeople cannot accumulate or carry evidence of good work. The existing remedies are either informal channels producing no durable information, or international platforms that do not operate locally and whose models assume infrastructure this market does not have.

The design responds to that problem in three connected parts. Discovery is made systematic through an AI diagnosis that recommends matching providers, backed by category and area search ordered by rating, price, and availability. The transaction is made legible through structured intake, AI-generated scheduling, a provider-confirmed parts list, quoting, and a visit model that accommodates jobs as they actually proceed rather than as an idealised single appointment. Reputation is made credible by tying every review to a job the system itself observed reaching completion, and by enforcing that tie in the schema rather than in the interface.
